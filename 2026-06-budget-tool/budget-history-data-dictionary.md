# Boulder city budget history — data dictionary & caveats

Generated by `budget-history.py`. Coverage: **2003–2027**, 348 observations across
84 measures, from 8 sources. Re-run the script to regenerate.

**Gap: 2007 and 2009 have no citywide total** (nor does 2003). Everything from
2004 to 2027 is covered apart from those two. Not zeros — genuinely absent:

- **2009** exists only as a scanned PDF, and no neighbouring book states its
  citywide total. Its General Fund and staffing figures *are* here, because the
  2011 book carries 2009 in its own comparison columns.
- **2007** is in a biennial 2006‑2007 book whose citywide summary block states
  2006 only. Its General Fund and staffing figures come from the 2008 book's
  four-year tables. The block for 2007, if it exists, is most likely on page 60
  of `2006-2007 Annual Budget, Volume 1.pdf`, which the page-text dump skipped.
- **2010** was a gap until this pass and no longer is: the 2011 book states its
  own total "represents a 0.38% increase over the 2010 approved budget", which
  pins 2010 to $230.14–230.17M. It is marked `derived`.

> **The single most important thing in this file:** there are **two accounting
> bases here and they must never be charted as one series.** The `budget_*`
> headline totals and the `sources_*`/`uses_*` citywide snapshot disagree by
> **$83.4M in 2023** for structural reasons — see caveat 8.

| File | Shape | Use |
|---|---|---|
| `budget-history.csv` | tidy long | the canonical data; one row per `(year, measure, basis)` |
| `budget-history-wide.csv` | wide | chart-ready headline series, each with its `*_basis` label |
| `budget-history-provenance.csv` | sidecar | one row per source document, joined on `source_id` |
| `budget-history-department-crosswalk.csv` | audit trail | one row per department line item per year: the label as its document printed it, the functional bucket it was assigned to, and why |

## Read this first: `basis` is load-bearing

A budget number means nothing without its vintage. The same year and measure
routinely carries three or four published values:

| 2025 sales & use tax | Value | Basis |
|---|---|---|
| What council adopted | $180.17M | `adopted` |
| Mid-year revision | $176.84M | `revised_projection` |
| What actually came in | $178.75M | `actual` |

Those differ by $3.3M — larger than the entire 2027 budget gap. **Filter to one
`basis` before charting.** A series that silently mixes them will look like
volatility when it is really just different questions.

| `basis` | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `actual` | Closed-year collections (2025 is unaudited year-end) |
| `adopted` | Approved by council for that year |
| `recommended` | City manager's proposal, not yet adopted (all 2027 figures) |
| `revised_projection` | Mid-year revision to an adopted figure |
| `total_budget` | Adopted **plus amendments and carryforward** (OpenGov snapshot, 2023) |
| `forecast` | Forward-looking model output |
| `identified` | A gap named during budget development |
| `derived` | **Computed here, not published** — see caveats |
| `policy` | A standing policy target, not a year-specific amount |

## Caveats

**1. 2025 General Fund is derived, not published.**
`budget_general_fund` for 2025 (`basis = derived`, $211.0M) was back-computed as
`194.5 / (1 − 0.078)` from the city's statement that 2026 fell 7.8% from 2025.
The 7.8% is itself rounded, so treat this as ±$1M and **do not quote it as a
city figure.** Every other General Fund value is published.

**2. Two different property-tax figures for 2026, and they are not errors.**
`property_tax_revenue` (adopted) is **$59.17M** — the city's own 11.648-mill
levy. `revenue_property_tax` (from the citywide revenue mix) is **$61.73M**,
a broader all-funds scope. They answer different questions; don't compare them
directly or chart them as one series.

**3. Component sums miss their published totals by a few hundredths.**
The city totals at full precision and publishes components rounded to $0.01M,
so a column of six components can legitimately miss its own total. These
residuals are recorded explicitly as `salesuse_component_residual` (range
−0.02 to +0.04) rather than hidden. The published total is authoritative.
`budget-history.py` fails the build if any residual exceeds $0.05M.

**4. Two different staffing measures — don't mix them.**
`positions_*` are year-over-year **changes** (eliminated, added, frozen) from
the council packets and press releases. `staffing_fte` is a citywide
**headcount level** from the budget books, which state it in prose: *"a total
city staffing level of 1,540.09 full-time equivalents."*

The level series covers **2016, 2018, 2021, 2022, 2023, 2025, 2026** only. A
missing year is a book that states no citywide figure on the pages scanned,
**not** a zero and not a gap in the city's staffing — do not interpolate across
them without saying so. The two families also don't reconcile arithmetically:
the changes are General Fund and departmental in scope while the level is
citywide, so subtracting one year's eliminations from the prior level will not
land on the next.

And there is a **third** staffing measure. `staffing_fte_standard` (2003–2011)
comes from the pre-2012 books' "Summary of Standard FTEs" tables, whose own
footnote limits the count to *standard* Management, BMEA, Fire and Police
positions plus standard positions funded through capital projects, grants and
internal service funds. The modern books say "citywide staffing level" with no
such qualifier. **No book in the corpus publishes both**, so the offset between
them cannot be measured — which is why they are separate measures and why
`staffing_fte_standard` jumping to `staffing_fte` across the 2011→2016 gap is
not a real 15% hiring spree. Finding one document that states both is the only
way to join them; until then, chart them as two series.

Within itself the standard series is solid: nine consecutive years, and every
year that appears in two books agrees exactly (2004 and 2005 in the 2005 and
2006‑2007 books, 2006 in the 2006‑2007 and 2008 books). Its shape is the
recession twice: 1,290.69 in 2003 → 1,200.68 in 2004, back to 1,288.52 by 2009,
down to 1,228.50 by 2011.

**4b. The `budget_*` series is one continuous NET measure, 2004–2027.**
I initially expected a basis break: the 2016 book publishes its total as "$327
million (excluding transfers)" against $515.4M for 2023, which looks like two
different things. The intervening years disprove it — 327, 322, 389, 354, 370,
342, 462, 515 is a continuous run — so the books report the same net measure
throughout and the 2016 phrasing is a scope description, not a separate series.
The gross counterpart is the `sources_*`/`uses_*` family (caveat 8).

Two independent checks support the run:

- **2022**: operating 300.1 + capital 162.4 = 462.5, exactly the stated total.
- **2005**: the 2005 book and the 2006‑2007 book each state $196,167,000, so
  that figure is confirmed by two separately published books.

`budget-history.py` enforces `operating + capital = total` for every year where
all three are known, and fails the build otherwise.

**4c. 2005–2017 operating includes debt service.** Those years come from a
summary block labelled "OPERATING BUDGET (including debt service)". The modern
operating figures don't obviously separate it out either, but only these say so
explicitly.

**4a. Book figures occasionally disagree with the later council packets.**
Both are "correct" — the book is what was published at adoption, the packet is
a later restatement:

| Year | Measure | Book | Packet |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2024 | capital | $140.7M | $141.2M |
| 2025 | total | $589.5M | $589.3M |

Where both exist the dataset keeps the packet value. The differences are small
but real; don't treat either as an error.

**5. Nominal dollars throughout.** No inflation adjustment. Year-over-year
changes across 2022–2027 partly reflect inflation, which was substantial early
in this window.

**6. All 2027 values are `recommended`.** Council's first reading was Oct 1,
2026 and final vote Oct 15, 2026. Figures may have changed on adoption — check
before publishing anything that depends on them.

**7. Coverage is uneven by measure, and the gaps are not the same gaps.**
`budget_total` runs 2004–2027 less 2007 and 2009. `budget_operating` and
`budget_capital` are thinner: capital is missing 2007, 2009–2011, 2013 and
2018–2021, because those books state a total and an operating figure but no
capital one. Check for the year you need rather than assuming a rectangle.

**8. Two accounting bases — do not merge them.**
The `sources_*`/`uses_*` family comes from the OpenGov citywide Sources & Uses
export and is **not** comparable to the `budget_*` headline totals:

| 2023 | `uses_expense_total` | `budget_total` | Gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| | $598.79M | $515.40M | **+$83.39M** |

Two structural reasons, not an error in either:

1. **Different vintage.** The snapshot's 2023 column is *Total Budget* —
   adopted plus amendments and carryforward — while $515.4M is the *adopted*
   figure. (The snapshot's 2022 column is adopted, and 2021 is actuals: three
   bases in one file, which is why each carries its own `basis`.)
2. **Gross, not net.** Interfund flows are left in on both sides — `transfers_in`,
   `intragovernmental_charges` and `cost_allocation` on the revenue side,
   `transfers` and `internal_services` on the expense side — so money moving
   between city funds is counted more than once. Removing just transfers and
   internal services closes about $75M of the $83M gap; the rest is the
   amendment/carryforward difference.

Charting the two families together produces a fake ~$83M cliff between 2023 and
2024. **Pick one family per chart.** Use `budget_*` for the headline story and
`uses_expense_*` for composition.

**9. Snapshot revenue categories are a different taxonomy** from the `revenue_*`
family (18 categories vs 11; the snapshot has `transfers_in`, `cost_allocation`
and `intragovernmental_charges`, which the 2026 mix has no equivalent for).
Don't join them on category name.

**10. Two General Fund measures, ~12% apart, and the difference is structural.**
`budget_general_fund` is **"Total General Fund Uses"**: General Fund
expenditures *plus* transfers out *plus* the 0.15% sales tax allocation. That is
what the books state in prose from 2012 on and what the modern council packets
report, so it is the series to chart.

`budget_operating_general` is something else — the General Fund *half of the
operating budget*, as printed in the 2005–2017 summary block, where

```
budget_operating_general + budget_operating_dedicated = budget_operating
```

holds to the thousand in all seven years. It runs consistently below the
all-in figure:

| Year | `budget_operating_general` | `budget_general_fund` | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2014 | $101.96M | $115.68M | 0.881 |
| 2015 | $113.55M | $128.48M | 0.884 |
| 2016 | $116.96M | $132.27M | 0.884 |

Substituting one for the other invents a ~12% step. That the all-in series is
continuous across the 2011→2012 change of document format is checked rather than
assumed: the 2012 book states its General Fund expenditures are "a 3.8 percent
increase over the total expenditures projected for the 2011 Approved Budget",
and 100.449 × 1.038 = 104.27 against the stated 104.234.

**Do not assume the smaller half is the General Fund.** It is, every year
through 2016 — and in 2017 the General Fund is the *larger* half ($130.86M
against $129.82M in dedicated funds). `budget-history.py` enforces the identity
and fails the build if the halves are swapped.

**11. The books' own year-over-year percentages do not always reproduce from
the books' own figures.** Where they disagree, the to-the-dollar figure is what
this dataset keeps. Most check out exactly — the 2015 and 2016 books' operating,
capital and total percentages all reproduce to the decimal — but three do not:

| Book | Says | Figures give |
|---|---|---|
| 2013 | total +9.2% over 2012 | +6.6% |
| 2014 | operating +4.6% over 2013 | +2.8% |
| 2017 | General Fund +4.9% over 2016 | +5.7% |

The likeliest explanation is a percentage computed against a revised or
amended base rather than the prior book's adopted figure. Either way, **use
the values, not the narrative percentages**, and don't treat a mismatch as an
extraction error.

**12. `revenue_total` has two points 15 years apart and one of them is
derived.** 2011 ($224.912M) is stated in the 2011 book; 2010 ($224.3M) is
derived from that book's "0.29% increase over the total revenues projected for
the 2010 approved budget"; 2026 ($507.2M) comes from the recommended-budget
packet. The 2011 book also prints a full eight-category citywide revenue mix on
the same page that sums exactly to $224,912 thousand — it is **not** in this
dataset, because its taxonomy matches neither the 2026 `revenue_*` categories
nor the OpenGov `sources_revenue_*` ones, and a third taxonomy would invite
exactly the false joins caveat 9 warns about. Page 68 of `2011 Annual
Budget.pdf` if you want it.

## Recipes

**pandas — one basis, wide for charting**

```python
import pandas as pd
df = pd.read_csv("budget-history.csv")

totals = (df[(df.measure == "budget_total") & (df.basis.isin(["adopted", "recommended"]))]
            .pivot_table(index="year", values="value"))

# Sales & use tax composition, actuals only
comp = (df[df.measure.str.startswith("salesuse_")
           & (df.measure != "salesuse_total")
           & (df.measure != "salesuse_component_residual")
           & (df.basis == "actual")]
          .pivot_table(index="year", columns="measure", values="value"))
```

**R / tidyverse**

```r
library(tidyverse)
df <- read_csv("budget-history.csv")
df |>
  filter(measure == "budget_total", basis %in% c("adopted", "recommended")) |>
  pivot_wider(names_from = measure, values_from = value)
```

**Straight to a chart.** Use `budget-history-wide.csv` — it is already one row
per year, and each series carries a `*_basis` column so a footnote can say which
vintage each point came from.

**13. The departmental series is bucketed, and the buckets are coarse on purpose.**
Boulder reorganized its departments repeatedly over twenty years, so raw labels
do not form a series. In 2005 **Public Works is a single $67.4M slice**; by 2024
that money is spread across Transportation and Mobility, Utilities, Facilities
and Fleet, and development review. The split is not recoverable — the 2005 book
divides Public Works three ways in its *staffing* table but not in its *spending*
pie. So `deptexp_*` uses seven buckets, coarse enough that the coarsest year
still maps:

| Bucket | Holds |
|---|---|
| `public_safety` | Police, Fire / Fire-Rescue, Police-Fire pensions |
| `infrastructure` | Public Works and all its successors: Transportation and Mobility, Utilities, Facilities and Fleet |
| `parks_openspace` | Parks & Recreation, Open Space (and Mountain Parks) |
| `community_services` | Housing & Human Services, Library, Arts |
| `planning_climate` | Planning & Development Services, Community Planning & Sustainability, Climate Initiatives |
| `administration` | Council, Manager, Attorney, Finance, HR, IT, Communications, Municipal Court, Administrative Services, General Government |
| `citywide_debt` | Debt service as published, Fundwide/Citywide |

**Every assignment is recorded per year in
`budget-history-department-crosswalk.csv`** — the label exactly as its document
printed it, the bucket, its share of that year's departmental total, and a note
wherever the call was a judgment rather than obvious (20 of the 84 rows carry
one). That file is *generated from* the assignments the script actually uses, so
it cannot drift from them, and anyone who disagrees with a bucket can re-bucket
from it without reopening the PDFs.

Two assignments to know about before charting:

- **`administration` and `citywide_debt` trade content across the series.** 2005's
  "General Government" ($17.3M) probably holds non-departmental items that
  2024–2026 books to "Fundwide / Citywide". It is the least comparable pair.
- **The published debt slice is General Fund debt only.** Both the 2005 and 2011
  pies note that non-General-Fund debt service sits inside the departments. The
  bucket is consistent across years, but it is not all of the city's debt service.

`budget-history.py` checks that the buckets plus any balancing line sum to
`budget_total`, and that a given label never changes bucket between years unless
its row carries a note.

**14. 2024–2026 departmental figures carry a $108–116M balancing line.**
The only departmental source for those years is an OpenGov cost-centre export
taken with a 23-fund filter that omits the utility, debt-service and
internal-service funds. Its twenty cost centres total $407.8M / $473.4M / $411.9M
against published citywide budgets of $515.4M / $589.3M / $521.0M, so the
shortfall is carried explicitly as `deptexp_funds_outside_export` (basis
`derived`, since it is the difference rather than a reported figure).

The giveaway is the "Utilities" cost centre at **$0.34M in 2026** — the utility
enterprise funds are outside the filter, so nearly all real utility spending is
in the balancing line. **One re-export of the same view with no fund filter
replaces that line with actual departmental detail, and is the single
highest-value download left for this dataset.**

**15. 2005 and 2006 have departmental detail; 2011 does not, and that is a
refusal rather than a gap.** The 2011 book prints the same citywide pie, but it
comes out of the PDF with labels and values interleaved beyond repair —
`DUHMD/ Housing/ Open Space/Prkng Svcs Human Svcs 9, 679 Mtn Prks Police 46
12, 964 24, 518 …` — and the legible values fall $46.6M short of the $231.0M
total. Pairing those labels to those values would be guessing, and a wrong guess
here does not look wrong: it silently moves Police's budget to Open Space. The
extractor's sum gate rejects the year instead. 2005 and 2006 pass the same gate
exactly, to the dollar, which is what makes them trustworthy.

## The three-stage book pipeline

The historical figures come out of the PDF budget books in three steps, and only
the third costs anything:

| Script | Does | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| `budget-books-inventory.py` | one row per PDF: year, pages, born-digital vs scanned, and which pages match each target. `--dump-text` writes the matched pages as JSONL | free |
| `budget-books-ocr.py` | sends the scanned books' summary pages to Datalab and writes JSONL in the same shape | ~$1.50 |
| `budget-books-extract.py` | reads any number of those dumps and emits candidate figures with the page each came from | free |

Six of the volumes are scans with no text layer at all — 1,651 pages in total:
2005 Vol 2, 2007 Vol 1 and 2, 2009 Vol 1 and 2, 2010 Vol 1. Almost none of that
is worth paying to read. 2005 Vol 1 is born-digital and already covers 2005;
Volume 2 is the capital program in every year of this corpus and carries
essentially no citywide summary pages (the readable 2006‑2007 Vol 2 matched one
summary keyword in 149 pages, the 2008 Vol 2 matched none in 207); and the
citywide summary block sits between page 58 and page 102 in every book whose text
we can read. So the OCR stage defaults to pages 50–115 of three Volume 1 scans —
198 pages — and `--estimate` prints the bill before anything is sent.

**The category pages need a wider dump than the one that built this.** The
keyword list that produced the current page dump was aimed at totals, staffing
and General Fund tables, and it does not reach the pages where the revenue mix
and the departmental spending split live — the 2012–2017 books head those
"Figure 4-2: Citywide Revenues (Sources) for 2012" and "Figure 5-06: Citywide
Expenditures for 2013". `budget-books-inventory.py` now carries a `categories`
target group for exactly those headings. Re-running the dump is free and local:

```
python3 budget-books-inventory.py ~/budget-books --dump-text dump.jsonl
python3 budget-books-extract.py dump.jsonl -o budget-books-extracted.csv
```

Until that runs, the departmental series covers **2005, 2006 and 2024–2026** and
the citywide revenue mix covers **2011 and 2026**. The missing middle is a dump
that never looked at those pages, not an absence in the books.

One trap worth knowing about, because it is silent: Datalab returns markdown, in
which a table becomes pipe-delimited rows with the year and the basis zipped
together per column (`| 2009 APPROVED | 2010 APPROVED |`). pypdf flattens the
same table by position, giving the years as one run and the basis words as
another. `budget-books-extract.py` handles both, but a new row type tested only
against one shape will quietly return nothing for the other, which looks exactly
like a year with no data. `budget-books-ocr.py --verify` runs the extractor over
a fresh OCR dump and prints the per-measure coverage for that reason.

## Extending this

**The cheapest route backwards is a book's own comparison columns.** Every
budget book before 2012 prints its summary tables three or four years WIDE, with
the basis of each column in the header:

```
CITY OF BOULDER / SUMMARY OF USES OF FUNDS (in $1,000s)
    2006       2007       2008       2009
  ACTUAL    APPROVED   APPROVED   PROJECTED
Total General Fund Uses   89,123   89,650   94,238   95,883
```

One page therefore yields four years, which is the only reason 2007, 2009 and
2010 have any values at all — the 2008 book states 2007, and the 2011 book
states 2009 and 2010, whose own books are scans. Reading a neighbouring book's
comparison columns is worth more than OCR'ing the missing book, and it is free.
`budget-books-extract.py` reads these automatically (`MULTIYEAR_TABLES`); adding
a row type is three lines.

**OpenGov exports CSV — this is the other route to deeper history.** The `snapshot2023`
source is a "Data Snapshot" CSV downloaded from an OpenGov transparency view.
That matters: the portal has no *public API* (documented REST paths return 404),
but each view exports clean, to-the-dollar CSV from the UI. Every additional
year is a download, not a PDF transcription.

To extend the series backward, open a Sources & Uses view for an earlier budget
year and export it the same way:

```
https://cityofboulderco.opengov.com/transparency/#/<dataset-id>
    ?accountType=revenuesVersusExpenses&breakdown=types&year=<year>
```

Each export carries three columns (prior-year actual, current adopted, current
total budget), so **one download adds up to three years**. The 2023 view
(dataset `65843`) reached back to 2021 actuals; a 2020 or 2017 view should reach
proportionally further.

Two things to watch when adding one:

- **Record the column labels as `basis`.** They differ per export — "Actual",
  "Adopted Budget", "Total Budget" are three different things (caveat 8).
- **Keep them in the `sources_`/`uses_` family.** Do not fold them into
  `budget_*`, however tempting the matching year looks.

**Still not available from OpenGov:** FTE *levels*. Sources & Uses carries
`uses_expense_personnel` (payroll dollars), which is a reasonable proxy for
staffing cost but is not a headcount. A true FTE series needs the personnel
schedules in the PDF budget books.

**Adding a year:** add the figures to the appropriate block in
`budget-history.py` with a `src` id, add the document to `SOURCES`, and re-run.
Reconciliation will catch a mistyped component — it checks every published total
against the sum of its parts, to the dollar for the snapshot data.
